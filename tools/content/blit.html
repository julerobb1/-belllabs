Blit

The Blit was a programmable bitmap graphics terminal designed at Bell Labs in the early 1980s. It was used with UNIX research systems and is an ancestor of the later graphics work on Plan 9. Internally it was also known as the Jerq.

A terminal that could draw

Most terminals of the 1970s displayed rows of characters. The Blit, designed by Rob Pike, Bart Locanthi, and colleagues at Bell Labs, was a microprocessor-based terminal with a bitmapped screen. Programs on the host (typically a UNIX system) could download code to the terminal over the serial line so that editing, windowing, and graphics ran on the Blit itself rather than as a stream of cursor-addressing characters.

The name is associated with bit-block transfer (“blit”), the operation used to move rectangles of pixels. The machine sat on a desk, connected to a time-shared UNIX host, and made that host feel more like a workstation years before workstations were cheap.

What followed

Ideas from the Blit—downloaded terminal programs, mouse-driven editing, overlapping windows—show up in later Bell Labs interfaces, including the 5620 DMD (Dot-Mapped Display) and the window systems around Plan 9 (8½ and rio) and the Acme editor. The Blit was a research and development terminal, not a Long Lines transmission product; it is here as part of Bell Labs computing history alongside UNIX.
