Microwave radio relay

Microwave radio relay was the over-the-air backbone of AT&T Long Lines from the 1950s into the 1980s. A chain of line-of-sight stations, typically 25 to 30 miles apart, carried thousands of telephone circuits and the television networks from city to city. The best-known Long Lines radio was TD-2, a 4 GHz system Bell Labs designed for that plant.

For the towers, horn-reflector antennas, and site buildings, see Microwave relay towers.

Why microwave

By the late 1940s the Bell System needed more long-distance capacity than open wire and early coaxial could provide quickly. Microwave frequencies—roughly a few gigahertz—can be formed into narrow beams with reasonably sized antennas, so a transmitter on one hill can talk to the next hill without a wire in between. The path must be line-of-sight, and rain or multipath fading can degrade a hop, so the radio routes were engineered with spare channels and, on important paths, backup routes.

Bell Labs’ wartime radar work and earlier experimental microwave links (including the New York–Boston experimental system of the late 1940s) led to a production long-haul radio: TD-2.

TD-2 and the transcontinental radio

TD-2 operated in the 3.7–4.2 GHz band. Each radio channel carried a large FDM baseband of telephone channels (and could carry a television signal). Repeaters were heterodyne: they shifted the incoming microwave to an intermediate frequency, amplified it, and translated it back to microwave for the next hop, rather than demodulating all the way to audio at every station.

AT&T opened a TD-2 route between New York and Chicago in 1950. On August 17, 1951, it completed the first transcontinental microwave system, New York to San Francisco, which carried both telephone traffic and television. That radio crossing of the continent is the event usually meant by “the first transcontinental microwave system” in Long Lines histories.

Later Long Lines radios added other bands and higher capacity, including 6 GHz TH systems and 11 GHz short-haul radios (TJ, TL, TM and related types). Stations often mounted several horns, each aimed at a different neighbor, so one hilltop was a junction rather than a simple two-way repeater.

Antennas and stations

The distinctive “sugar scoop” or “cornucopia” on so many surviving towers is a horn-reflector antenna (Western Electric KS-15676 and related types). A horn feeds a section of paraboloid, combining high gain with a shielded feed that reduced noise from the ground and from rain on the reflector. Waveguide ran from the radio bays in the building up to the horns.

Each station had an equipment building with radios, multiplex, power, and batteries or generators. Alignment of the horns was a surveyor’s job: a fraction of a degree of tilt could drop a hop out of the network. Ice, wind, and tower twist were standing design problems.

What the network carried

Long Lines microwave carried message telephone, leased private lines, and the AT&T television network that interconnected the broadcast networks. Protection switching could move traffic onto a spare radio channel in a fraction of a second if a working channel faded or a transmitter failed. On the busiest routes, 1+1 “hot standby” radios were used.

The radio network and the L-carrier coaxial network were planned as a single long-distance plant. A call from New York to Los Angeles might ride radio for some sections and cable for others, under the same switching hierarchy described in Switching systems.

Retirement

Fiber-optic cables, which AT&T began installing in quantity in the 1980s, offered more bandwidth and did not depend on line-of-sight or radio licenses. Most Long Lines microwave was taken out of service in the 1980s and 1990s. Towers were sold, re-used for cellular and other radio services, or demolished. What remains is documented by preservationists and by sites such as this gallery and independent maps at long-lines.com.
