AMPS

The Advanced Mobile Phone System (AMPS) was the analog cellular telephone standard Bell Labs designed for the United States. It was the first generation of cellular service: frequency-division radios, hexagonal cells, and handoff from cell to cell as a car moved.

Cellular, not a bigger mobile phone

Pre-cellular mobile telephone in the Bell System used a handful of high-power channels in a city and could serve only a small number of subscribers. Bell Labs’ cellular idea reused the same frequencies in geographically separated cells, with a switch (originally the Mobile Telephone Switching Office) tracking the mobile unit. That architecture is described in Bell Labs papers of the 1960s and 1970s; the production analog air interface was AMPS.

The FCC authorized commercial cellular service in 1982. Illinois Bell opened the first U.S. AMPS system in Chicago on October 13, 1983. Other Bell operating companies and radio common carriers followed in the mid-1980s. AMPS occupied 800 MHz spectrum (later also 1900 MHz digital overlays in other standards).

What AMPS was, technically

AMPS was analog FM voice with digital signaling for call setup and handoff. Cell sites connected back to a mobile switching office, which in turn connected to the landline network—including, for long-distance, the same AT&T network that Long Lines had built. Digital systems (D-AMPS/TDMA, later GSM and CDMA) replaced analog AMPS; analog AMPS service in the United States was shut down in the 2000s.

AMPS is a Bell Labs systems invention in the same sense as ESS: a network architecture, not a single device. It belongs on this site because it is how Bell Labs extended switching and radio into personal mobile telephony.
