Telstar

Telstar 1 was the first active communications satellite to relay live television, telephone, and facsimile across the Atlantic. AT&T financed the project; Bell Labs designed and built the spacecraft and the U.S. ground station; NASA launched it. The satellite went into orbit on July 10, 1962.

An AT&T satellite on a NASA rocket

By the late 1950s Bell Labs was already studying satellite relays, including the passive Echo balloon (a NASA spacecraft that reflected microwave signals). An active satellite would receive a signal, amplify it, and retransmit it—far more useful for telephony and television, but it required space-qualified electronics just a few years after the first transistors and solar cells.

Telstar was a commercial AT&T project flown on a NASA Delta rocket from Cape Canaveral. Europe participated through ground stations: notably Goonhilly Downs in Cornwall (General Post Office) and Pleumeur-Bodou in Brittany (France). The American terminal was the Andover Earth Station in Andover, Maine, whose large horn antenna sat under an inflated radome.

The spacecraft

Telstar 1 was a small spherical satellite, about a meter in diameter, covered with solar cells that charged nickel-cadmium batteries. It carried a microwave repeater: signals received in one band were amplified by a traveling-wave tube and retransmitted in another. The satellite was spin-stabilized. Because it flew in an elliptical low-to-medium Earth orbit—not a geostationary orbit—a given ground station could see it for only about 20 minutes at a time, so live transatlantic television had to be scheduled for those passes.

Bell Labs also used the satellite and the Andover horn for radio-propagation experiments. The same laboratory had already built the Holmdel Horn Antenna that Penzias and Wilson later used in the discovery of the cosmic microwave background; the Andover horn was a much larger communications antenna for the Telstar service.

First transatlantic television

Telstar 1 began relaying test signals almost immediately after launch. Public demonstrations of transatlantic television followed, including a widely reported exchange on July 23, 1962, that showed images from the United States to European viewers and from Europe to the United States. Telephone, telegraph, and facsimile were also demonstrated. Those broadcasts were the first time live television had crossed the ocean by satellite.

The orbit meant the service was intermittent. Geostationary satellites—proposed by Arthur C. Clarke and later flown by other organizations, including Hughes and Intelsat—would keep a spacecraft fixed in the sky and allow continuous coverage. Telstar proved that an active repeater in orbit could handle television and telephony; later systems solved the scheduling problem.

Radiation damage and Telstar 2

Telstar 1’s electronics were damaged by radiation in the Van Allen belts. The high-altitude nuclear test Starfish Prime, detonated on July 9, 1962—the day before Telstar’s launch—had intensified that environment. Command problems appeared within months; Bell Labs engineers temporarily recovered the satellite by workarounds from the ground, but Telstar 1 failed for good in early 1963.

Telstar 2, a similar Bell Labs satellite with improved radiation tolerance, was launched on May 7, 1963. It continued experimental transatlantic relays. Neither Telstar was a standing commercial telephone plant in the way L-carrier or TD-2 was; they were proof that satellites belonged in the long-distance toolkit. Regular commercial satellite telephony and television soon moved to geostationary fleets, including Intelsat, in which AT&T was an early participant through Comsat.

What remains

Telstar 1 is still in orbit as inert hardware. The Andover station was later used for other satellite work; the radome and horn are part of the standard Telstar story in museum and documentary accounts. Nokia Bell Labs and AT&T have both marked the anniversary of the 1962 launch in public histories.

This site does not host film of the 1962 broadcasts. Contemporary footage is held by broadcast archives and is often shown in documentaries; look there rather than in this repository.
