L-carrier

L-carrier was the Bell System’s family of analog coaxial-cable systems for long-distance telephone and television. From the early 1940s through the 1970s, L-carrier routes formed the underground complement to the microwave radio network: high-capacity, weather-resistant trunks buried along highways and railroads, with unmanned repeaters every few miles.

This page covers the L-carrier systems as a whole. A companion article, Coaxial cable systems, describes construction, power feed, and how the cables were laid.

What “L-carrier” means

In Bell System naming, a “carrier” system stacks many telephone channels onto one transmission path by frequency-division multiplexing (FDM). The L designation was used for coaxial-cable carrier, as distinct from open-wire carrier (older K and J systems) and later digital T-carrier on paired cable. A coaxial tube—a center conductor inside a cylindrical shield—can carry a very wide band of frequencies with relatively low radiation and interference, which is why it was chosen for these high-capacity analog routes.

Each successive L system used a wider frequency band, closer repeater spacing, and more coaxial tubes in the cable, so that one buried sheath could carry thousands of voice circuits or several television channels.

The L-system series

Capacities below are the conventional figures published in Bell System engineering literature for a working coaxial pair (two tubes, one in each direction). Exact circuit counts varied with multiplex plan and whether television occupied part of the band.

System First used Nominal voice capacity Notes
L1 1941 480 telephone channels, or one television channel First commercial coaxial carrier. Early route work included New York–Philadelphia. Repeaters were vacuum-tube amplifiers several miles apart.
L3 1953 1,860 telephone channels, or television plus telephony Used on transcontinental coaxial routes in the 1950s. Improved amplifiers and equalization.
L4 1967 3,600 telephone channels Transistorized repeaters; smaller-diameter cable designs; higher reliability than the tube-era systems.
L5 1974 10,800 telephone channels on a coaxial pair Last major analog coaxial system. An enhanced L5E arrangement raised capacity further on existing L5 routes. Solid-state repeaters and a dense FDM hierarchy.

There was no production “L2” long-haul system in the same series; L3 followed L1 as the next widely deployed long-distance coaxial carrier. Cables themselves often contained many tubes (commonly eight, twelve, or twenty in later sheaths), so a single buried route could carry several working coaxial pairs plus protection pairs.

Repeaters, power, and the buried plant

Analog coaxial signals fade with distance and frequency. L-carrier therefore depended on regularly spaced repeaters. Spacing tightened as bandwidth grew: on the order of several miles for early L1, down to about a mile on L5. Many repeaters sat in buried manholes or small huts and were powered over the cable from power-feed stations tens of miles apart, so a local electric outage did not immediately silence the route.

Equalization—correcting the cable’s frequency response as temperature and age changed—was a major engineering problem. Later systems added automatic equalization so that a coast-to-coast stack of amplifiers would still meet noise and distortion limits.

How L-carrier fit the Long Lines network

Microwave radio (especially TD-2) was faster to build across open country and did not require a continuous buried right-of-way. Coaxial L-carrier was preferred where high capacity, secrecy, or immunity to radio fading mattered, and on routes that could follow railroads and highways. By the 1960s and 1970s the two media were used together: radio and cable in parallel, with protection switching between them.

Television network distribution used both media. Before satellites were routine, a transcontinental television program might ride microwave in some sections and L-carrier coaxial in others.

Beginning in the late 1970s and 1980s, lightwave (fiber-optic) systems took over the same rights-of-way. Fiber offered still greater capacity and did not need the analog FDM hierarchy. Most L-carrier electronics have been retired; the ducts, manholes, and buildings often remain in use for fiber.

L-carrier is not T-carrier

T1 and later T-carrier systems are digital, originally 24 pulse-code-modulated voice channels on paired cable (1.544 Mbit/s). L-carrier is analog FDM on coaxial tubes. Both were Bell Labs / Western Electric products used by Long Lines and the operating companies, but they are different plants. Digital coaxial systems (such as T4/T5 on coax, and later lightwave) eventually displaced analog L on many routes.
