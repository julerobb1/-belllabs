AT&T Network Operations Center

As the Long Lines network grew into a national web of radio, coaxial, and switching machines, AT&T needed a place from which engineers could see the whole of it. The Network Operations Center (NOC) was that place: a continuously staffed room that watched trunk groups, radio channels, and switches, and that could intervene when traffic or failures threatened the network.

Bedminster

The best-known AT&T operations center is at Bedminster, New Jersey. It opened in 1977 as a purpose-built network management facility for the long-distance plant. Contemporary descriptions and photographs show a large wall display of the United States and overseas routes, with consoles for surveillance and control. From there, staff could observe switching machines such as the No. 4 ESS, the status of transmission systems, and the effect of storms, cable cuts, or overloads.

Regional network management centers handled a smaller geography; Bedminster was the national (and, for overseas services, international) overview. The methods—alarm collection, traffic measurements, and manual or automatic controls that restrict new calls to protect calls already up—grew out of Bell Labs traffic engineering and from earlier, smaller operating rooms in the Long Lines department.

What the NOC actually did

  • Surveillance. Telemetry from switches and transmission systems was summarized so that a failure in a radio hop or a cable was visible as an event, not only as scattered customer complaints.
  • Traffic control. During peak days (holidays, disasters) the network could be ordered to block overflow, skip certain routes, or protect essential services. Those controls were the operational side of the hierarchical switching plan described on Switching systems.
  • Coordination. Restoration after a cut—rerouting traffic onto intact radio or cable, dispatching field forces—was directed from the operations centers working with Long Lines plant staff.

Common Channel Interoffice Signaling (and later SS7) made some of this faster, because signaling no longer shared the voice trunk. The NOC remained a human organization: displays and computers did not replace the people who decided when to apply a control.

After divestiture

The 1984 breakup of the Bell System split local and long-distance operations. AT&T kept a long-distance network and continued to run a network operations center. The building and the idea—one room that represents a continental network—became a template other carriers copied. The original Long Lines radio and L-carrier plant that those screens once depicted has largely been replaced by fiber.

Film and photographs

Period documentaries and news features filmed the Bedminster display wall. This site does not host those films. If you want moving pictures, look for broadcast-archive copies or AT&T historical reels rather than a file in this repository. Photographs of the center appear in AT&T annual reports and in contemporary technical magazines.
