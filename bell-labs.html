Bell Labs

Bell Telephone Laboratories—usually called Bell Labs—was the research and development organization of the Bell System. From its founding in 1925 through the breakup of AT&T in 1984, it was one of the most productive industrial laboratories in the world, combining basic science with the engineering needed to build a nationwide telephone network.

This site is an independent historical tribute. It is not affiliated with AT&T Inc. or with Nokia Bell Labs, the present-day successor organization.

An industrial research laboratory

Bell Labs was created in 1925 when AT&T and Western Electric consolidated their research work into a single company, Bell Telephone Laboratories, Inc. Its job was both scientific and practical: understand the physics of communication, then turn that understanding into equipment the Bell System could manufacture and operate.

The laboratory’s best-known campus was at Murray Hill, New Jersey, opened after World War II. Other major sites included Holmdel, Whippany, and locations in New York City. Researchers there worked on everything from radio propagation and switching theory to solid-state physics, computer science, and radio astronomy.

Because the Bell System was a regulated monopoly for most of the twentieth century, Bell Labs could support long-term research that did not have to pay off in a single product cycle. That arrangement produced both incremental improvements to the telephone plant and discoveries whose effects reached far outside telephony.

What Bell Labs is known for

A short list cannot cover the laboratory’s output, but these are among the results most often cited in histories of the institution:

  • The transistor (1947), which replaced vacuum tubes in electronics and earned John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics.
  • Claude Shannon’s 1948 paper “A Mathematical Theory of Communication,” which founded information theory and introduced the bit as a unit of information.
  • The laser, following Charles Townes and Arthur Schawlow’s optical maser proposal (Townes later shared a Nobel Prize for maser/laser work).
  • The silicon photovoltaic cell demonstrated in 1954 by Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller, and Gerald Pearson.
  • The charge-coupled device (CCD), invented in 1969 by Willard Boyle and George E. Smith, later recognized with the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics.
  • The 1964 discovery of the cosmic microwave background radiation by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, using a Horn Antenna at Holmdel—work awarded the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics.
  • UNIX and the C programming language, developed from the late 1960s by Ken Thompson, Dennis Ritchie, and colleagues; later, Plan 9 from Bell Labs.
  • Telstar, the AT&T/Bell Labs communications satellite launched in 1962.
  • Electronic and digital switching, including the No. 1 ESS and later ESS machines that replaced electromechanical offices.
  • Cellular telephony: Bell Labs developed the architecture that became AMPS, the first-generation U.S. analog cellular system.

Work done at Bell Labs has been recognized with multiple Nobel Prizes in Physics. The laboratory also received Turing Awards and other major scientific honors for computing and communications research.

Bell Labs and Long Lines

AT&T Long Lines was the long-distance operating arm of the Bell System. Bell Labs designed much of the transmission and switching plant that Long Lines built: microwave radio relay such as TD-2, L-carrier coaxial cable systems, undersea cables, and the toll switches that sat in regional centers. The two organizations were distinct—one researched and developed, the other constructed and operated—but the network visitors still photograph today is largely Bell Labs engineering put into the field by Long Lines and Western Electric.

After the 1984 divestiture, Bell Labs became part of the new AT&T, then of Lucent Technologies, Alcatel-Lucent, and today Nokia. Long Lines as a Bell System division ended with divestiture; the physical microwave and coaxial plant was later retired in favor of fiber optics.
